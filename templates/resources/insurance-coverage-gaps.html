Insurance Coverage Gaps: How Expired Policies Cost You Contracts

A lapsed insurance policy can kill a deal faster than a bad quote. Here's what causes coverage gaps, how certificates of insurance work, and how to track every policy expiration so it never happens to you.

What is a coverage gap?

A coverage gap is any period when you're running a business or holding a contract without active insurance coverage. It can be as short as a day — a policy that expired yesterday and renews tomorrow — or as long as months, if you forgot to renew entirely.

Coverage gaps matter for two reasons:

  • You're exposed. If something happens during the gap — a liability claim, a property loss, a workers' comp incident — you pay out of pocket.
  • You're contractually non-compliant. Most B2B contracts, commercial leases, and client agreements require you to maintain specific insurance coverage. A gap is a breach, and the other party can terminate or withhold payment.

What causes coverage gaps

In practice, coverage gaps rarely happen because someone decided to skip insurance. They happen because of process failures:

  • Renewal notices went to an old address or an old email that no one monitors.
  • The person who handled insurance left the company and the renewal calendar went with them.
  • Payment failed silently — a card expired, a bank detail changed, the direct debit bounced.
  • Someone assumed auto-renewal when the policy actually required active renewal.
  • The broker changed carriers and issued a new policy with a start date that didn't match the old one's end date.
  • You switched brokers and there was a handover gap.

Every one of these is a tracking failure, not an insurance failure. Which means every one is fixable with the right system.

Certificates of insurance (COIs) and why they matter

If you work with vendors, contractors, or subtenants, you've probably been asked for a certificate of insurance — a one-page document from the vendor's insurer proving they carry general liability, workers' comp, auto liability, or other coverage.

COIs have expiration dates. When a vendor's COI expires, you may be:

  • Contractually required to stop them from working on your site.
  • Liable if they cause damage while uninsured on your property.
  • In violation of your own insurance policy's terms.

Most companies collect COIs once at onboarding and never check them again. That's a compliance gap waiting to happen. The right approach is to track every COI's expiry date and request a renewed certificate 30–60 days before it lapses.

The types of policies to track

Depending on your business, this list may be short or long. Track them all:

  • General liability — required by almost every B2B contract and lease.
  • Professional liability / E&O — required for consultants, agencies, and service providers.
  • Workers' compensation — required by law in most jurisdictions if you have employees.
  • Commercial auto — required if any vehicle is used for business purposes.
  • Commercial property — covers premises, equipment, and inventory.
  • Cyber liability — increasingly required for any company handling client data.
  • Directors & officers (D&O) — required for many corporate boards and investors.
  • Umbrella / excess liability — required for larger contracts above primary limits.

Then add the vendor-side policies you need to track — the COIs from every contractor, subcontractor, and landlord-adjacent party whose insurance you rely on.

How to track insurance expirations

1. Centralise every policy end date in one list

Not in three different brokers' emails. Not in a spreadsheet only one person can access. One list, with: policy type, insurer, policy number, end date, and renewal contact.

2. Set reminders 90, 60, and 30 days before expiry

Broker turnaround for renewal quotes is often 2–6 weeks. Starting 90 days out gives you time to shop alternatives if the renewal quote is bad — which, given how insurance markets have moved, is often worth doing.

3. Track COI expirations separately

Your own policies and your vendors' certificates of insurance are two different lists. Both need tracking. For COIs, a 60-day reminder is usually enough — vendors can usually produce a renewed certificate within a few days of request.

4. Audit annually

Once a year, review every policy: is the coverage still right for your business? Has your revenue or headcount changed enough to warrant higher limits? Are there new coverage types you should carry that you didn't need a year ago (cyber, E&O)? Insurance isn't a set-and-forget thing.

The cost of getting it wrong

A single coverage gap can cost more than a decade of insurance premiums:

  • A liability claim during a gap: uncapped, out of pocket.
  • A lost contract because your COI lapsed: the contract value, plus the relationship.
  • A workers' comp incident during a gap: fines, legal exposure, and potential criminal liability in some jurisdictions.
  • A property loss during a gap: full replacement cost.

Tracking expiry dates is cheap. Not tracking them is not.

Frequently asked questions

What is an insurance coverage gap?

A coverage gap is any period when you're running a business or holding a contract without active insurance coverage, whether that's a single day or several months.

What causes most insurance coverage gaps?

Process failures, not decisions to skip coverage: renewal notices going to an old address, the person who handled insurance leaving the company, a silently failed payment, or an assumption that a policy auto-renews when it doesn't.

What is a certificate of insurance (COI)?

A certificate of insurance is a one-page document from a vendor's insurer proving they carry coverage such as general liability or workers' compensation. COIs have their own expiration dates that need separate tracking.

How far in advance should I track insurance renewals?

Set reminders at 90, 60, and 30 days before expiry for your own policies, since broker turnaround for renewal quotes is often 2-6 weeks. For vendor COIs, a 60-day reminder is usually enough.
